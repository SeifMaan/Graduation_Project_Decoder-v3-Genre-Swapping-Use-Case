CHAPTER 4
The Weight of Crowns and Choices

**A Quiet Morning in the Spring Court**

The world blurred at the edges as we stepped forward—one moment, the scent of snow and pine; the next, the thick, cloying perfume of blooming jasmine and damp earth. My boots sank into plush carpet instead of frozen ground, and the air, humid and heavy, clung to my skin like a second layer of clothing. I blinked, disoriented, as the light shifted from the silver-gray of dawn to the golden haze of morning in the Spring Court.

Tamlin was already gone.

I stood alone in the center of the bedroom, the familiar four-poster bed draped in emerald silk, the vanity cluttered with jars of cream and perfume bottles I’d once used without thought. The room was exactly as I’d left it—except for the layer of dust on the windowsill, the way the sheets on the bed were still tucked tight, untouched since the last time I’d slept here.

My chest tightened.

I rubbed at my arms, the thick charcoal sweater suddenly stifling. The silver-and-sapphire band on my left hand glinted in the sunlight streaming through the windows, a reminder of vows spoken in another life. Or maybe this one. Time had a way of folding in on itself lately.

A knock at the door made me jump.

“Come in,” I called, voice rough from disuse.

The door creaked open, and a young faerie maid—one I didn’t recognize—peered inside, her ears twitching nervously. “Lady Feyre,” she said, bowing so low her forehead nearly brushed her knees. “The High Lord requests your presence in the gardens for breakfast.”

I exhaled through my nose. *Of course he does.* “Tell him I’ll be down in a minute.”

She hesitated. “He—he said it was urgent.”

I bit back a sharp retort. Tamlin had always been bad at waiting. “Fine. I’ll come now.”

The maid scurried off, her bare feet silent on the marble floors. I took a moment to steady myself, running my fingers through my loose braid to smooth the worst of the tangles. The mirror over the vanity showed a face I barely recognized—pale, with dark circles under my eyes, the faintest dusting of freckles across my nose from too many days under the sun. I looked like I hadn’t slept in weeks.

Because I hadn’t.

I grabbed my coat from the back of a chair, the red-and-gray scarf Mor had given me still tucked inside the pocket. The fleece-lined boots were a small mercy; at least my feet wouldn’t freeze on the way to the gardens.

The halls of the Spring Court were eerily quiet.

No laughter, no music, no chatter of courtiers. Just the distant hum of servants moving through the wings, their voices hushed, their eyes darting away when they saw me. The usual scent of fresh bread and honey was absent, replaced by something sharper—fear, maybe, or the acrid tang of old magic.

I clenched my fists in my coat pockets.

The gardens were worse.

The once-vibrant blooms were wilting, their petals curled and brown at the edges. The fountain in the center of the courtyard, which should have been burbling merrily, was silent, its basin cracked and empty. Tamlin stood beneath the skeletal branches of the apple tree, his golden hair dull in the weak sunlight, his green eyes fixed on the horizon.

He turned as I approached, and for a second, something like relief flickered across his face. Then it was gone, replaced by that careful, guarded expression he’d worn since—

Well. Since everything.

“You’re late,” he said.

I arched a brow. “I was told it was urgent.”

A muscle feathered in his jaw. “I didn’t want to eat alone.”

I almost laughed. Almost. “Since when do you care about eating alone?”

His gaze darkened, and I immediately regretted the words. Tamlin had always been proud, but the male standing before me now was a shadow of the High Lord I’d once known—his shoulders tense, his hands restless at his sides, as if he couldn’t decide whether to reach for me or shove them in his pockets.

I sighed. “Tam—”

“Eat,” he interrupted, gesturing to the table laden with food. “Before it gets cold.”

I sat, because what else was I supposed to do? The spread was impressive—fresh fruit, warm bread, honey cakes, a platter of sliced meats—but my stomach twisted at the sight of it. I picked at a strawberry, the juice too sweet on my tongue.

Tamlin didn’t touch his food. He just watched me, his fingers drumming against the table. “You look tired.”

I swallowed the strawberry whole. “I *am* tired.”

“You’ve been gone a long time.”

“Not long enough, apparently.”

His mouth thinned. “That’s not what I meant.”

“I know.” I set the half-eaten strawberry down. “But it’s the truth.”

Silence stretched between us, thick and suffocating. The wind rustled the dead leaves above us, a sound like dry bones rattling. I shivered.

Tamlin’s voice was quiet when he spoke again. “Do you regret it?”

The question caught me off guard. “Regret what?”

“Coming back.”

I stared at him, at the way the light caught the gold flecks in his eyes, at the tension in his shoulders, the way his fingers dug into the arms of his chair. He looked like a male bracing for a blow.

And I realized, with a sickening lurch, that he was.

I exhaled slowly. “No,” I said at last. “I don’t regret coming back.”

A lie. Or maybe not. The truth was more complicated than that.

Tamlin’s shoulders relaxed, just a fraction. “Good.”

I reached for a piece of bread, tearing it absently. “Why did you really ask me to breakfast?”

He didn’t answer right away. When he did, his voice was careful, measured. “The court is… uneasy.”

“Uneasy,” I repeated flatly. “That’s one word for it.”

“They don’t understand why you’re here.”

“Neither do I, half the time.”

That earned me a sharp look. “Feyre.”

I dropped the bread onto my plate. “What do you want me to say, Tamlin? That I’m thrilled to be back? That I’ve missed this place?” I gestured to the dying garden, the silent fountain, the empty halls. “Because I haven’t.”

His hands curled into fists. “Then why did you come?”

“Because I *had* to.” The words tasted bitter. “Because someone had to make sure this court didn’t implode while you were too busy brooding in your study.”

Tamlin’s eyes flashed. “I was *protecting* this court.”

“By locking everyone out?” I shot back. “By letting the gardens rot and the people starve?”

“You don’t understand—”

“Then *make* me understand!” I slammed my hands on the table, rattling the dishes. “Because right now, it looks like you’re more interested in wallowing than ruling.”

Tamlin surged to his feet, his chair scraping against the stone. “You think I *wanted* this?” he snarled. “You think I *chose* to be this—this *monster*?”

The word hung between us, heavy and final.

I stood slowly, my heart hammering. “No,” I said quietly. “I don’t.”

Tamlin’s chest heaved, his breath coming in sharp, uneven bursts. For a moment, I thought he might reach for me. For a moment, I thought I might let him.

But then the wind shifted, carrying with it the scent of something foul—rot, decay, the stench of old magic gone wrong. Tamlin’s head snapped toward the trees, his eyes widening.

“What is that?” I whispered.

His face went pale. “We’re not alone.”

And then the screaming started.

---

**Confrontation in the Garden**

The screaming tore through the night like a blade through silk.

I whirled toward the sound, my pulse hammering in my throat, but Tamlin was already moving—shoving me behind him with a snarl, his golden eyes flashing in the dim light of the garden lanterns. His hand shot out, magic flaring at his fingertips, and for a heartbeat, the world narrowed to the sharp, coppery tang of fear in the air.

Then the screaming stopped.

Silence rushed in, thick and suffocating. Tamlin’s shoulders remained rigid, his breath coming fast, but the garden itself seemed to hold its breath. The roses—once vibrant, now wilting at the edges—dripped with dew that glistened like scattered diamonds in the moonlight. The scent of damp earth and crushed petals clung to the air, cloying and sweet.

I stepped out from behind him, my boots sinking slightly into the soft soil. “What in hell was that?”

Tamlin didn’t look at me. His gaze was fixed on the shadows between the hedges, where the lantern light didn’t quite reach. “Nothing good.”

I huffed, crossing my arms. “That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only one you’re getting.” His voice was low, rough, like gravel underfoot.

I opened my mouth to argue, but the words died on my tongue as his eyes finally flicked to mine. The warmth that had once lived there was gone, replaced by something colder. Harder.

“You’re back,” he said, and it wasn’t a greeting.

I lifted my chin. “I am.”

A muscle twitched in his jaw. “For how long?”

The question hung between us, heavy as the silver-and-sapphire band on my left hand—the one he’d once placed there with trembling fingers. I resisted the urge to touch it. “I don’t know.”

His laugh was sharp, bitter. “Of course. Why would you?”

I exhaled through my nose, my fingers curling into fists at my sides. “Tamlin—”

“You left.” The words were a blade, quick and precise. “You left, and you didn’t look back.”

The garden seemed to tilt around me. The roses, the lanterns, the very air—all of it suddenly too bright, too loud. I could feel the weight of his accusation pressing down on my chest, but I refused to let it crush me.

“I had to go,” I said, my voice steadier than I felt. “You know why.”

“Do I?” He stepped closer, his boots kicking up a spray of damp earth. “Because from where I stood, it looked like you chose them over us. Over *me*.”

Them. The word was a spark in the dark, and I felt the heat of it lick up my spine. “That’s not fair.”

“Fair?” His laugh was a jagged thing. “You want to talk about fair, Feyre? How about the fact that I spent months wondering if you were dead? How about the fact that I had to hear from *Rhysand* that you were alive and well and playing court in the Night Court?”

I flinched. The name was a slap. “That’s not—”

“Not what?” he snapped. “Not true? Not what I deserve?”

I swallowed hard, my throat tight. The garden was too quiet, the air too still. Even the roses seemed to be holding their breath. “I never meant to hurt you.”

“But you did.” His voice broke, just for a second, and the sound of it sent a pang through my chest. “You left, and you didn’t even say goodbye.”

I wanted to reach for him. To take his hand and press it to my cheek and tell him I was sorry, that I hadn’t known how to stay. But the words stuck in my throat, tangled in the thorns of everything unsaid.

“I had to go,” I repeated, softer this time. “I couldn’t—”

“Couldn’t what?” he demanded. “Couldn’t stand the sight of me? Couldn’t bear to be in the same court as the male who *broke you*?”

The air left my lungs in a rush. “That’s not—”

“Don’t lie to me.” His voice was a growl, his hands clenching at his sides. “Not about this.”

I stepped back, my boots crunching on a fallen branch. The sound was too loud, too final. “I’m not lying.”

“Then what is it?” he pressed, following me. “What was so terrible here that you had to run to *him*?”

The question was a punch to the gut. I could feel the heat rising in my cheeks, the old anger flaring bright and hot. “You don’t get to do this,” I said, my voice shaking. “You don’t get to act like I betrayed you when you—”

“When I *what*?” he roared, his magic flaring around him in a sudden, violent gust. The lanterns flickered, their light casting jagged shadows across his face. “When I tried to protect you? When I did everything I could to keep you safe?”

“You *chained* me!” The words tore out of me, raw and bleeding. “You locked me away like some fragile thing, like I was made of glass and not bone and blood and *rage*!”

Silence.

The garden seemed to shudder around us, the roses trembling in the sudden stillness. Tamlin’s chest heaved, his eyes wide and wild. “I was trying to keep you alive.”

“By killing me first?” I shot back. “By making me feel like I was already dead?”

He flinched like I’d struck him. For a moment, neither of us moved. Neither of us breathed. The weight of everything unsaid pressed down between us, heavy as the crowns we both carried.

Then, quietly, he said, “You really think that’s what I wanted?”

I didn’t answer. I couldn’t.

The wind picked up, rustling the leaves, and the scent of roses wrapped around me—sweet and suffocating. Tamlin’s gaze dropped to the ground, his shoulders slumping. “You’re different,” he murmured.

I swallowed hard. “So are you.”

He looked up then, his eyes searching mine. “Is it too late?”

The question hung between us, fragile as the petals underfoot.

I didn’t know the answer.

But for the first time in a long time, I let myself hope.

---

**A Message from the Night Court**

The question lingered in the air between us, sweet and sharp as the scent of crushed violets underfoot. *Is it too late?*

I didn’t answer. Not because I didn’t know—because I did, in some deep, unshakable part of myself—but because the words felt too fragile to speak aloud. Hope was a dangerous thing in the Spring Court. It had a way of wilting before it ever took root.

A gust of wind slithered through the garden, carrying the bite of winter and the distant chime of the palace bells. I pulled my coat tighter, the fleece lining warm against my skin, and exhaled, watching my breath curl into the cold. The scarf Mor had given me last winter—red and gray, soft as a whisper—brushed against my chin as I turned toward the palace.

That’s when I saw him.

A shadow detached itself from the twilight, moving with the eerie grace of the Night Court. My pulse jumped, but I didn’t reach for the dagger at my thigh. Not yet. The figure wore the livery of Rhys’s personal guard—a high-collared coat of deepest indigo, embroidered with silver stars that seemed to shift when the light hit them just right. His face was obscured by a hood, but the way he carried himself, the way the air hummed around him—*power, but leashed*—told me he wasn’t here to start a fight.

He stopped a respectful distance away, hands clasped behind his back. “Lady Feyre.”

The voice was smooth, cultured, and just familiar enough that I relaxed a fraction. “You’re one of the Illyrians from the war council,” I said. “Kael, right?”

A faint smile touched his lips. “You have a good memory.”

“Hard to forget someone who tried to stab my mate in the back.” I arched a brow. “Metaphorically, of course. Rhys still hasn’t forgiven you for that training exercise.”

Kael’s smile widened, sharp as a blade. “He’s never let me live it down. Though, in my defense, he *did* cheat.”

I snorted. “Rhys? Cheat? Never.”

The wind picked up again, tugging at the loose strands of my braid, and I tucked them behind my ear. “What are you doing in the Spring Court, Kael? And more importantly—” I gestured to the sealed envelope in his hand. “—is that for me?”

He held it out, the paper thick and creamy, the wax seal stamped with Rhys’s sigil—a crescent moon cradling a star. “His Highness requested your immediate attention.”

Immediate. The word settled in my stomach like a stone.

I broke the seal, the wax snapping under my fingers, and unfolded the letter. Rhys’s handwriting was elegant, precise, but the words sent a chill down my spine.

*Little wolf,*
*We have a problem. The Suriel’s visions have taken a darker turn, and the Court of Nightmares is stirring. I need you here. Not as my mate, not as High Lady—but as my partner. The Spring Court’s troubles are yours to manage, but this… this is bigger than borders or thrones. Come home. Bring the others if you can, but come quickly.*
*Yours,*
*R.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something less urgent.

Kael shifted, his boots crunching on the frost-kissed gravel. “He didn’t say much more than that. Only that time was of the essence.”

I folded the letter carefully, my mind racing. The Spring Court was still healing—still *fractured*—after the war. Tamlin was a shadow of himself, the people were restless, and the nobles… well, the nobles were vultures waiting for the first sign of weakness. If I left now, it could undo everything.

But Rhys wouldn’t have sent for me if it wasn’t important.

I looked up at Kael. “How long do I have?”

“He didn’t specify. But if I were you, I wouldn’t dawdle.”

I exhaled sharply, rubbing my temples. “Right. Of course not.”

Kael tilted his head, studying me. “You’re considering staying.”

It wasn’t a question, but I answered anyway. “I have responsibilities here.”

“And there,” he countered. “Rhysand doesn’t ask for help often. When he does…” He let the implication hang.

I knew what he meant. Rhys was many things—infuriating, brilliant, stubborn—but he wasn’t reckless. If he was calling me back, it was because he needed me. *Not* because he wanted to pull me away from my duties.

I closed my eyes, the weight of the choice pressing down on me. The Spring Court needed me. But the Night Court—*Rhys*—needed me more.

“Tell him I’m coming,” I said at last. “And that he owes me. Again.”

Kael’s grin was all teeth. “I’ll be sure to relay the message.”

I turned back toward the palace, my boots crunching on the path. “I’ll need to gather the others. And pack.”

“Feyre.”

I paused, glancing over my shoulder.

Kael’s expression had softened, just a little. “He’s waiting for you.”

The words settled in my chest, warm and heavy. I nodded once, then strode toward the palace, the letter burning a hole in my pocket.

Rhys was waiting.

And I was coming home.

---

**Preparations and Farewells**

The gravel crunched beneath my boots as I strode toward the palace, the letter from Rhys a searing weight in my pocket. *Home.* The word settled in my chest like a promise—or a threat. I hadn’t decided which yet.

The Spring Court’s golden light spilled across the manicured lawns, too bright, too cheerful, as if the world itself hadn’t just cracked open beneath my feet. The air smelled of blooming jasmine and damp earth, the scent cloying, suffocating. I tugged the gray coat tighter around me, Mor’s scarf—red and gray, like a bruise—wrapped snugly against the morning chill. The fabric still held the faintest trace of her perfume, something dark and spiced, like cloves and midnight. It steadied me.

Inside, the palace was a tomb of gilded silence. Servants moved like ghosts, their eyes sliding away from me as I passed. I didn’t blame them. Not after everything.

Tamlin’s study door was ajar, golden light spilling into the hall. I knocked once, then pushed it open without waiting for an answer.

He stood by the window, his back to me, the morning sun gilding the edges of his golden hair. His shoulders were rigid, his hands clasped behind him like a soldier at parade rest. The tension in the room was thick enough to choke on.

“You’re leaving,” he said, voice flat.

I shut the door behind me. “Rhys sent a letter. There’s something I need to handle in the Night Court.”

Tamlin turned then, and the sight of his face—hollowed out, shadows under his eyes—made my chest tighten. He looked like he hadn’t slept in weeks. Neither of us had, really.

“Of course he did,” he said, bitterness lacing his words. “Always with the cryptic bullshit.”

I bristled. “It’s not cryptic. It’s *important.*”

“Right.” He scoffed, running a hand through his hair. “Because the Night Court’s problems are always more urgent than ours.”

“That’s not fair.”

“Isn’t it?” His eyes burned into mine, green and gold and furious. “You’re my wife, Feyre. Or have you forgotten that?”

The silver-and-sapphire band on my left hand suddenly felt like a shackle. I flexed my fingers, resisting the urge to yank it off. “I haven’t forgotten anything.”

Silence stretched between us, taut as a bowstring. The clock on the mantel ticked, each second a tiny hammer against my ribs.

Tamlin exhaled sharply, some of the fight draining out of him. “When will you be back?”

“I don’t know.”

“You never do.”

I swallowed. “Tamlin—”

“Just go.” He turned back to the window, his reflection in the glass a stranger’s. “Before I change my mind about letting you.”

The words stung more than they should have. I opened my mouth, closed it. What was there left to say?

I turned on my heel and left.

---

The stables were quiet, the scent of hay and horse warm and familiar. My mare, a dappled gray named Briar, nickered softly as I entered her stall. She nudged my shoulder, her breath hot against my neck.

“Missed me, huh?” I murmured, scratching behind her ears. She huffed, as if to say, *Obviously.*

I saddled her quickly, my movements efficient, practiced. The weight of the dagger at my hip was a comfort. Just in case.

Outside, the courtyard was empty except for Lucien, leaning against the stone wall with his arms crossed. His red hair glinted in the sunlight, his golden eye sharp as ever.

“You’re really doing this, then?” he said, pushing off the wall. “Running off to the Night Court again?”

I swung into the saddle. “I’m not *running.*”

“Sure you’re not.” He smirked, but there was no humor in it. “You know, for someone who’s supposed to be the High Lady of the Spring Court, you’re not very good at sticking around.”

I glared down at him. “And for someone who’s supposed to be my friend, you’re not very good at being supportive.”

Lucien’s smirk faltered. He rubbed the back of his neck, suddenly looking younger, more vulnerable. “I *am* your friend, Feyre. That’s why I’m telling you this.” He stepped closer, lowering his voice. “Tamlin’s not handling this well. None of us are.”

I looked away, my throat tight. “I know.”

“Do you?” He reached up, gripping the reins just below my hands. “Because from where I’m standing, it looks like you’re choosing *him* over us. Again.”

The words hit like a physical blow. I yanked the reins free. “It’s not like that.”

“Then what’s it like?”

I didn’t have an answer.

Lucien sighed, stepping back. “Just… be careful, all right? The Night Court’s not exactly known for its hospitality.”

“I’ll be fine.” I nudged Briar forward, but Lucien caught my arm.

“Feyre.” His voice was quieter now, serious. “If you don’t come back—”

“I *will* come back,” I said firmly.

He studied me for a long moment, then nodded. “Good.”

I urged Briar into a trot, the wind tugging at my braid as we left the courtyard. The palace gates loomed ahead, wrought iron and gold, the Spring Court’s sigil gleaming in the sunlight. Beyond them, the road stretched into the distance, a ribbon of possibility—or peril.

I didn’t look back.

---

The forest swallowed me whole.

The trees here were ancient, their bark gnarled and twisted, their leaves a riot of emerald and gold. The air was cooler beneath their canopy, the sunlight dappled and weak. I breathed in deep, the scent of pine and damp earth grounding me.

Then I felt it.

A prickle at the back of my neck, like fingers trailing down my spine. Someone was watching me.

I slowed Briar to a walk, my hand drifting toward the dagger at my hip. The forest was too quiet. No birds, no rustling leaves. Just… silence.

“Who’s there?” I called, my voice steady despite the hammering of my heart.

No answer.

I turned in the saddle, scanning the trees. Nothing. But the feeling didn’t fade.

*Paranoia,* I told myself. *You’re just on edge.*

But I knew better.

I nudged Briar into a canter, my pulse quickening. The road ahead was empty, but the weight of unseen eyes followed me all the same.

And for the first time in a long time, I wondered if I was truly ready for what waited in the Night Court.
