CHAPTER 2
Shadows of the Past

**Morning Aftermath**

The world was a drum, and someone had taken a mallet to the inside of Feyre’s skull.

She groaned, rolling onto her side—only to freeze as the movement sent a fresh wave of nausea crashing over her. The bed beneath her was too soft, too warm, the sheets tangled around her legs like vines. Not her bed. Not her room.

*Where the hell—?*

Memory flickered, sluggish and fragmented. Wine. Too much wine. Rhysand’s smirk. His voice, low and smooth, wrapping around her like smoke. *"One night. No strings."*

Her stomach lurched. She bolted upright—or tried to. The room spun, and she flopped back onto the pillows with a whimper.

The cabin. They were in the cabin.

Feyre dragged a hand over her face, wincing as her fingers brushed the tender skin beneath her eyes. She could still taste the wine on her tongue, bitter and cloying. The last thing she remembered was stumbling through the snow, Rhysand’s arm slung around her waist, his laughter warm against her ear.

And now—now she was alone.

The realization settled over her like a cold draft. She peeled back the blankets, her breath fogging in the chilly air, and swung her legs over the edge of the bed. The floorboards creaked beneath her bare feet, the sound too loud in the quiet. She was still wearing the heavy charcoal sweater from last night, the fabric rumpled and smelling faintly of pine and Rhysand’s spice-and-shadow scent.

Her leggings were gone. She glanced down, relieved to find she was still wearing underwear. Small mercies.

The cabin was dim, the morning light filtering through the curtains in pale gold streaks. The fire in the hearth had burned down to embers, casting long, wavering shadows across the walls. Feyre rubbed her arms, the chill seeping into her bones, and padded toward the kitchen.

A mug sat on the counter, half-full of cold coffee. Beside it, a plate with a single slice of buttered toast, now stale. A note, folded in half, rested against the mug.

Her fingers trembled as she picked it up.

*Feyre,*

*Gone to fetch breakfast. Try not to burn the place down while I’m gone.*

*—Rhys*

She exhaled, a laugh bubbling up despite the pounding in her head. Of course he’d leave a note like that. Of course he’d be gone when she woke up, like some kind of thief in the night.

She set the note down and leaned against the counter, pressing her palms into the cool wood. The cabin was too quiet. Too still. The last time she’d been here, it had been alive with laughter, with the crackle of the fire and the clink of glasses and Rhysand’s voice, low and teasing, in her ear.

Now, it felt like a ghost of itself.

She wandered toward the living area, her fingers trailing over the back of the couch, the edge of the coffee table. A wineglass sat abandoned on the side table, its rim stained with the deep red of last night’s vintage. A throw blanket was draped haphazardly over the armchair, as if someone had tossed it aside in a hurry.

Her chest tightened.

She shouldn’t be here. This place was a minefield of memories—of stolen kisses and whispered confessions, of the way Rhysand had looked at her like she was the only thing in the world that mattered. And then, later, of the way he’d walked away.

The front door creaked open.

Feyre whirled around, her heart leaping into her throat—just as Rhysand stepped inside, shaking snow from his dark hair. He stilled when he saw her, his violet eyes flickering with something unreadable.

“You’re awake,” he said, his voice carefully neutral.

Feyre crossed her arms. “And you’re back.”

A beat of silence. Then Rhysand held up a paper bag, the scent of warm bread and bacon wafting from it. “Brought breakfast.”

She eyed the bag, her stomach growling traitorously. “You left a note.”

He smirked, setting the bag on the counter. “I didn’t want you to think I’d abandoned you.”

“Too late.”

The words slipped out before she could stop them. Rhysand’s smirk faltered, just for a second, before he turned away, busying himself with unpacking the food.

Feyre exhaled sharply through her nose. *Stupid. So stupid.* She shouldn’t have said that. Shouldn’t have let him see how much it still stung.

But then Rhysand spoke, his voice quiet. “I didn’t think you’d want to wake up to me hovering.”

She swallowed. “I didn’t think you’d care either way.”

He stilled. Then, slowly, he turned to face her. The morning light caught the angles of his face, the sharp line of his jaw, the way his shadows curled at his feet like living things.

“Feyre,” he said, and her name on his lips sent a shiver down her spine. “I care.”

The air between them was thick, charged. Feyre’s pulse pounded in her ears, her fingers curling into the fabric of her sweater.

Then Rhysand cleared his throat, breaking the spell. “Eat something. You’ll feel better.”

She wanted to argue. Wanted to press him, to demand answers, to *scream* at him for leaving, for coming back, for making her feel like this—like she was standing on the edge of a cliff, one wrong move away from falling.

But her head was still throbbing, and the scent of bacon was making her mouth water, so she just nodded.

Rhysand handed her a plate, their fingers brushing for the briefest moment. A spark. A warning.

She ignored it.

For now.

**A Visitor from the Past**

The wind howled through the pines, sharp as a blade and just as relentless. It sliced through the thick wool of my coat, sending a shiver down my spine despite the layers—Mor’s scarf wrapped tight around my throat, gloves snug over my fingers, the charcoal sweater clinging to my frame like a second skin. I exhaled, watching my breath curl into the frigid air before dissipating into nothing.

*A warning.* The words echoed in my skull, but I shoved them aside. Not now. Not when the weight of the world already pressed down on my shoulders like a physical thing.

I needed space. Just a moment to breathe, to think, to *not* be the High Lady of the Night Court. The cabin had become a sanctuary of sorts, its walls thick with the scent of aged wood and the faint, lingering trace of cedar and frost—Rhys’s scent. But even here, the quiet was suffocating.

I stepped off the porch, my fleece-lined boots crunching over the thin layer of snow that dusted the ground. The forest stretched before me, a sea of skeletal branches clawing at the gray sky. Somewhere beyond the trees, the world was moving, shifting, plotting. And here I was, hiding in the middle of nowhere like a coward.

“You look like hell.”

The voice cut through the silence, smooth and rough all at once, like gravel wrapped in velvet. I didn’t need to turn to know who it was. The way my pulse jumped, the way my fingers twitched toward the dagger at my thigh—*that* was all Cassian.

I turned slowly, schooling my features into something resembling indifference. “And you look like you’ve been hitting the training rings a little too hard. Your face is doing that thing where it forgets how to smile.”

Cassian stood a few paces away, arms crossed over his chest, wings tucked tight against his back. His dark hair was windswept, his jaw shadowed with stubble, and his golden eyes burned with something between amusement and exasperation. “You’re deflecting.”

“And you’re trespassing.”

He smirked, unfazed. “This is Night Court territory. Technically, *I’m* the one who should be complaining.”

I rolled my eyes, but the corner of my mouth betrayed me, lifting just a fraction. “What do you want, Cassian?”

He sobered, the smirk slipping away like it had never been there at all. “To make sure you’re not doing something stupid.”

“Define *stupid*.”

“Ignoring the fact that the entire court is on edge. That Rhys is barely holding it together. That *you* are out here brooding like some tragic heroine in a bad play.”

I bristled. “I’m not brooding.”

“You’re standing in the snow, staring at the trees like they hold the answers to the universe. That’s the definition of brooding.”

I opened my mouth to argue, but the words died on my tongue. Because he wasn’t wrong. And that irritated me more than anything.

Cassian took a step closer, his boots crunching in the snow. “Feyre. We need you.”

The weight of that statement settled over me, heavy and unyielding. “Rhys is—”

“Rhys is doing his best,” Cassian cut in, voice low. “But he’s not *you*. The court needs its High Lady. Not just in name, but in *action*.”

I swallowed hard, the cold air burning my throat. “I can’t just—”

“You can,” he said, firm. “And you will. Because that’s what you do. You face the impossible, and you *win*.”

I wanted to argue. Wanted to tell him that it wasn’t that simple, that the weight of everything—of the war, of the court, of the future—was too much. But the look in his eyes stopped me. It wasn’t pity. It wasn’t even sympathy. It was *faith*.

And that was worse.

I exhaled sharply, my breath fogging between us. “What’s really going on, Cassian?”

He hesitated, just for a second, before his expression darkened. “There’s something coming. Something big. And it’s not just the human queens or Hybern this time.”

My stomach twisted. “What do you mean?”

“I mean,” he said slowly, “that the world is shifting. And not in a good way.”

A gust of wind tore through the trees, sending snow swirling around us like a ghostly dance. I pulled my coat tighter, my fingers numb inside my gloves. “You’re being cryptic.”

“And you’re being stubborn.”

I shot him a glare. “I’m not stubborn. I’m *careful*.”

“Careful is good,” he conceded. “But careful doesn’t win wars.”

I wanted to scream. Wanted to throw something. Wanted to *not* feel like the ground was crumbling beneath my feet.

Cassian must have seen it—the crack in my composure—because his voice softened. “Feyre. We need you. *Rhys* needs you.”

I closed my eyes, the wind biting at my cheeks. “I don’t know if I can do this.”

“Bullshit.”

My eyes snapped open.

He grinned, sharp and unrepentant. “You’ve done harder things. You’ll do this too.”

I wanted to believe him. Wanted to believe that I wasn’t just a girl from the woods, playing at being a queen.

But the weight of the court, of the future, pressed down on me all the same.

Cassian stepped closer, his voice dropping to a murmur. “You’re not alone in this.”

I met his gaze, the golden flecks in his eyes catching the dim light. “I know.”

And for the first time in days, I almost believed it.

**Unfinished Business**

The cabin door clicked shut behind me, sealing out the winter wind and the weight of the world beyond. I exhaled, my breath fogging in the chill air, and shrugged off my heavy coat. The wool scarf—Mor’s, judging by the faint scent of vanilla and spice clinging to it—slid from my neck, pooling on the worn wooden floor like a crimson river.

I flexed my fingers inside my gloves, feeling the ache in my knuckles from the cold. The cabin was quiet, the kind of quiet that pressed against your ears, broken only by the crackle of the dying fire in the hearth and the distant whisper of snow against the windows. My boots thudded softly as I crossed the room, shedding layers like armor.

The easel stood in the corner, still draped in the ghost of my last attempt—a half-finished landscape of the Night Court’s mountains, the colors too muted, the strokes too hesitant. I ran a finger along the edge of the canvas, dusting off a smudge of dried paint. My chest tightened.

*Unfinished business.*

That’s what this was. What *he* was.

I grabbed a fresh canvas from the stack against the wall, the rough texture biting into my palms. The scent of turpentine and linseed oil filled my nose as I squeezed tubes of paint onto the wooden palette—ultramarine, cadmium red, titanium white. My fingers moved on instinct, mixing colors, testing strokes. The brush was a familiar weight in my hand, the bristles splaying slightly as I dragged it through the paint.

The first stroke was always the hardest. A slash of blue across the blank expanse, bold and unapologetic. Then another. And another. The scene took shape beneath my hands—a storm-wracked sky over the Hewn City, the spires of the palace piercing the clouds like blackened bones. I painted the rain, the way it slanted sideways in the wind, the way it blurred the edges of the world.

But it wasn’t the city I was really painting.

It was *him.*

The way his shadows curled at the edges of my vision. The way his voice dropped to that low, velvet rumble when he was teasing me. The way his fingers had brushed against mine last night, so careful, like I was something fragile.

I huffed a laugh, shaking my head. Fragile. Right.

The brush stilled in my hand. Beneath the fresh layers of paint, something glinted—a fleck of gold, peeking through the storm clouds. I frowned, leaning closer. My pulse thudded in my throat.

I set the brush down and reached for a rag, dabbing at the spot. The paint smeared, revealing more—swirling letters, half-hidden beneath the blue. My breath hitched.

*Feyre.*

Just my name, scrawled in his handwriting, the ink shimmering faintly, as if lit from within. I traced the letters with my fingertip, the warmth of the magic seeping into my skin.

And then I saw it.

Beneath my name, another line, smudged as if written in haste.

*I’m sorry.*

My chest constricted. I scrubbed harder at the paint, revealing the rest of the message in fits and starts.

*I’m sorry I didn’t tell you sooner. I’m sorry I let you think I didn’t care. I’m sorry I’m not better at this.*

The words blurred as my vision swam. I swallowed hard, blinking rapidly.

*You’re the only thing that’s ever made me feel like I wasn’t just a monster wearing a pretty face.*

A laugh bubbled up in my throat, half-hysterical. Of course he’d hidden this. Of course he’d buried his feelings under layers of paint and sarcasm and shadows.

I sat back on my heels, staring at the canvas. The storm clouds I’d painted now looked like a veil, torn away to reveal the truth beneath.

The door creaked open behind me.

I didn’t turn. I didn’t need to.

“You found it, then,” Rhysand said, his voice low.

I could hear the smile in his words. The *nervousness.*

I picked up my brush, dipping it into the paint. “Took you long enough to say something.”

A pause. Then the soft rustle of his coat as he stepped closer. “I was working up to it.”

I swirled the brush in slow, deliberate circles. “And how’s that going for you?”

His laugh was warm, wrapping around me like a blanket. “Not as well as I’d hoped.”

I finally turned, meeting his gaze. The golden flecks in his eyes glinted in the firelight, bright against the violet. He looked… tired. Not the kind of tired that sleep could fix, but the kind that came from carrying too much for too long.

“You could’ve just *told* me,” I said, gesturing to the canvas. “Instead of leaving cryptic messages in my paint.”

He smirked, but it didn’t reach his eyes. “Where’s the fun in that?”

I set the brush down, wiping my hands on my leggings. “Rhys.”

His name hung between us, heavy with everything we weren’t saying.

He exhaled, running a hand through his dark hair. “I know. I should’ve—” He cut himself off, shaking his head. “I was scared.”

The admission hung in the air, raw and unguarded.

I stood, crossing the space between us in three strides. My hands found his, my fingers lacing through his. His skin was warm, the callouses on his palms rough against mine.

“So was I,” I admitted.

He looked down at me, his thumb brushing over the silver-and-sapphire band on my left hand. “You? Scared?” His voice was teasing, but his eyes were serious. “I find that hard to believe.”

I rolled my eyes. “Oh, shut up.”

He laughed, the sound rich and deep, and pulled me closer. His arms wrapped around me, his chin resting on the top of my head. I breathed him in—night and wind and something uniquely *him.*

“I’m still scared,” he murmured against my hair.

I tilted my head back, meeting his gaze. “Me too.”

His lips quirked. “We’re a pair, aren’t we?”

I grinned. “The worst.”

He leaned down, his forehead resting against mine. “The best,” he corrected softly.

And for the first time in days, I *believed* it.

**A Choice to Make**

The world narrowed to the space between us—the warmth of his breath, the rough scrape of his stubble against my cheek, the way his fingers tightened just slightly at my waist as if he, too, feared this moment might slip away like smoke.

“The best,” Rhysand corrected softly, his voice a low rumble against my lips. And for the first time in days, I *believed* it.

Then the door to the cabin burst open.

Cold air rushed in like a slap, carrying with it the scent of snow and pine and something sharper—Nesta’s perfume, that infuriating blend of frost and steel. My sister stood in the doorway, her silver eyes glinting with the kind of irritation that could curdle milk, her dark hair whipping around her face in the wind.

“Well,” she drawled, leaning against the doorframe with the kind of effortless grace that made me want to throw a boot at her. “This is *adorable*.”

Rhys didn’t so much as flinch. He just turned his head, one brow arched in amusement. “Nesta. Lovely to see you. Though I can’t say the same for your timing.”

She smirked, pushing off the frame and stepping inside. The door swung shut behind her with a definitive *thud*. “If I’d waited another five minutes, I’d have walked in on something that would’ve scarred me for life.”

I groaned, pressing my forehead into Rhys’s chest. His laugh vibrated against my skin, warm and rich, and I could practically *feel* the smugness radiating off him. “Traitor,” I muttered.

He just kissed the top of my head before pulling back, his fingers lingering at my jaw. “I’ll give you two a moment.” A glance at Nesta. “Try not to kill each other.”

Nesta rolled her eyes. “No promises.”

The moment the door clicked shut behind him, the temperature in the room dropped by ten degrees. Nesta crossed her arms, her gaze sweeping over me—my rumpled sweater, my loose braid, the way my coat was still half-draped over the back of the chair like I’d been too distracted to put it on properly.

“You look like hell,” she said.

I barked a laugh. “And you look like someone dragged you here against your will.”

“They did.” She kicked at a stray log near the hearth, sending up a shower of sparks. “Cassian’s idea. Said you were ‘moping’ and that I was the only one who could ‘drag your arse back to reality.’” She mimicked his deep voice with alarming accuracy, then shot me a look. “I told him he was an idiot, but here I am.”

I exhaled, rubbing my temples. The fire crackled between us, the scent of burning cedar thick in the air. “I wasn’t moping.”

“You were *hiding*.”

The word hung there, sharp and undeniable. I opened my mouth to argue, then shut it. Because she wasn’t wrong. The cabin, the quiet, the way Rhys had looked at me like I was the only thing that mattered—it had been a refuge. A place where the weight of the world couldn’t reach me.

But the world had a way of finding you, no matter how far you ran.

Nesta must have seen the realization flicker across my face because her expression softened—just a fraction. “You can’t stay here forever, Feyre.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?” She stepped closer, her boots thudding against the wooden floor. “Because from where I’m standing, it looks like you’re trying to pretend none of it ever happened. The war, the bargain, the *crown*.” Her gaze flicked to the silver-and-sapphire band on my left hand, then back to my face. “You’re High Lady of the Night Court now. That’s not something you can just *ignore*.”

I swallowed. The words tasted like ash. “I’m not ignoring it.”

“Then what are you doing?”

The fire popped, sending up a plume of embers. Outside, the wind howled, rattling the windows like it, too, was demanding an answer. I thought of the dreams that had haunted me for weeks—blood and screaming and the crushing weight of failure. I thought of the way my hands had shaken the first time I’d tried to wield the power of the Court of Nightmares, the way I’d nearly drowned in it.

I thought of Rhys, his voice in the dark: *You are not alone.*

“I’m scared,” I admitted quietly.

Nesta stilled. For all her sharp edges, for all the ways she pushed and prodded, she knew what it was to be afraid. She knew what it was to have the world carve you open and leave you bleeding.

She didn’t offer empty comfort. She never did. Instead, she said, “So am I.”

I blinked. “What?”

She shrugged, kicking at the rug. “You think I *want* to be here? You think I *want* to be the one dragging you back to the real world?” A bitter laugh. “I’d rather be anywhere else. But someone has to, and apparently, it’s me.”

I studied her—the tension in her shoulders, the way her fingers twitched at her sides like she was fighting the urge to reach for me. Nesta had never been good at this. At *caring*. But she was trying. For me.

“Why?” I asked softly.

She shot me a look. “Because you’re my sister. And because if I have to listen to Cassian whine about how you’re ‘wasting your potential’ one more time, I’m going to throw him off the House of Wind myself.”

I laughed, the sound rough but real. “That’s the only reason?”

She hesitated. Then, quieter: “No.”

The firelight danced across her face, casting shadows in the hollows of her cheeks. For a moment, she looked young. Vulnerable. “I don’t want to lose you,” she said. “Not to the war, not to the Court, not to *this*.” A gesture at the cabin, at the life I’d been trying to carve out for myself. “You’re the only family I have left.”

The words hit me like a physical blow. I thought of our father, broken and begging. Of our sisters, lost to the woods. Of the way Nesta had stood beside me in the ruins of Under the Mountain, her sword in hand, her face set in defiance.

I reached for her. She stiffened, but didn’t pull away as I tugged her into a hug. She smelled like frost and steel and home.

“You’re not going to lose me,” I murmured against her hair.

She let out a shaky breath. “Good. Because I *hate* crying.”

I laughed, pulling back to wipe at my eyes. “You’re terrible at this.”

“And you’re terrible at *hiding*.” She nudged me toward the door. “Now, are you coming back, or do I have to carry you?”

I glanced at the cabin—the cozy warmth, the promise of quiet. Then I looked at my sister, at the stubborn set of her jaw, the fire in her eyes.

I took a deep breath. “Let’s go home.”

Nesta smirked. “Took you long enough.”

And as we stepped out into the snow, the wind biting at our cheeks, I knew she was right.

I couldn’t hide forever.

But I didn’t have to face the world alone.
