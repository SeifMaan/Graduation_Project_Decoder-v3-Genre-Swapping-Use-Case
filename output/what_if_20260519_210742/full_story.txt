**Chapter 1: Scene 1**

The air in the alley was thick with the scent of damp brick and something sharper—ozone, maybe, or the metallic tang of old blood. Elias crouched in the shadows, his breath shallow, fingers curled around the hilt of the knife at his belt. The cobblestones beneath him were slick with rain that had fallen earlier, the puddles reflecting the flicker of gas lamps from the street beyond.

He wasn’t supposed to be here.

But then, he wasn’t supposed to be *anywhere* anymore.

A rat skittered past his boot, its claws clicking against the stone. He didn’t flinch. He was used to things that scuttled in the dark. What made his pulse spike was the sound of footsteps—slow, deliberate—echoing from the mouth of the alley.

Elias pressed himself against the wall, his shoulder blades digging into the rough mortar. The footsteps stopped. A match flared, casting a brief, golden glow over a face he knew too well.

*Veyra.*

She leaned against the alley’s edge, the match cupped in her palm, the flame licking at her fingertips before she shook it out. The scent of sulfur joined the other smells of the night. “You’re late,” she said, her voice low, amused.

Elias exhaled through his nose. “I didn’t agree to meet you.”

“No?” Veyra pushed off the wall, stepping into the alley proper. Her boots made no sound on the wet stone. “Then what are you doing here, *little ghost*?”

The nickname grated. He wasn’t a ghost. Not yet. But he didn’t correct her. Instead, he straightened, letting his hand fall away from the knife. “I’m leaving the city.”

Veyra laughed, a sound like breaking glass. “Oh, Elias. You’ve been *leaving* the city for three years now.” She tilted her head, her dark eyes glinting in the dim light. “Funny how you always end up back in the same alleys.”

He clenched his jaw. She wasn’t wrong. But this time was different. This time, he had a ship waiting at the docks, a name scratched onto a manifest, a life that didn’t involve looking over his shoulder every five seconds.

“What do you want, Veyra?”

She took another step closer, close enough that he could see the scar bisecting her eyebrow, the way her lips curled into something that wasn’t quite a smile. “I want you to work for me.”

Elias barked out a laugh. “No.”

“No?” She arched a brow. “Just like that?”

“Just like that.”

Veyra sighed, as if he were a child refusing to eat his vegetables. “You’re making a mistake.”

“Then it’s mine to make.”

For a long moment, she just stared at him. Then, with a suddenness that made him tense, she reached into her coat and pulled out a folded slip of paper. She held it out between two fingers. “Take it.”

Elias didn’t move. “What is it?”

“A name. An address.” She wiggled it slightly. “A job.”

“I don’t want it.”

“You don’t even know what it is.”

“I don’t care.”

Veyra’s expression darkened. “You *should* care.” She stepped forward, pressing the paper into his palm. Her fingers were cold. “Because if you don’t take this job, Elias, you’re going to find out exactly how little your life is worth.”

He looked down at the paper, his thumb brushing over the ink. A name. *Lirien Veyne.* An address in the merchant quarter. And beneath it, a single word: *Alive.*

His stomach twisted.

Veyra stepped back, her boots clicking against the stone. “You have until dawn. After that, the offer expires.” She turned, her coat flaring out behind her like wings. “And so, I suspect, will your luck.”

Elias didn’t watch her leave. He stared at the paper, the ink smudged in places, as if written in haste. The name meant nothing to him. But the word beneath it—*alive*—meant everything.

Because he knew what happened to people who crossed Veyra.

And he knew what happened to people who *didn’t.*

The rat from before darted past his feet again, this time pausing to sniff at the hem of his coat. Elias exhaled, crumpling the paper in his fist.

Dawn was in four hours.

He had a choice to make.

Chapter 2

**Chapter 2 | Scene 1**

The paper in Elias’s fist was damp with sweat, its edges biting into his palm like the teeth of a small, furious animal. He exhaled, the breath curling into the cold air of the stable loft, where the scent of hay and leather clung to everything. Below, the horses shifted in their stalls, their hooves scraping against packed earth, their warm bodies radiating the last of the day’s heat. The barn was quiet except for the occasional rustle of straw, the whisper of a tail swishing against wood.

Four hours until dawn.

Elias unfolded his fingers one by one, smoothing the crumpled paper against his thigh. The ink had bled in places where his grip had been too tight, but the words were still legible. *Meet at the old mill. Midnight. Come alone.* No signature, no seal—just the stark, demanding script of a man who didn’t need to sign his name to be recognized.

Or a trap.

He crumpled it again, tighter this time, as if he could squeeze the decision out of it. The mill was half a day’s ride from Black Hollow, deep in the woods where the trees grew so thick they blotted out the sky. A good place to disappear. A better place to die.

A floorboard creaked behind him.

Elias didn’t turn. He didn’t need to. The scent of pine and gunpowder preceded her, sharp and familiar. “You’re still here,” he said.

Seraphine dropped onto the hay bale beside him, the movement effortless, like a cat settling into a sunbeam. She was all lean muscle and restless energy, her dark hair tied back in a knot that was already coming undone. A smudge of soot streaked her cheekbone, and her fingers—long, capable—tapped against the hilt of the knife sheathed at her hip.

“Where else would I be?” she asked, voice dry. “You look like a man who’s about to do something stupid.”

Elias barked a laugh, short and humorless. “That’s rich, coming from you.”

She grinned, quick and bright, but it didn’t reach her eyes. “I prefer the term *reckless*. There’s a difference.” Her gaze flicked to the paper in his hand. “That the note?”

“Mm.”

“And?”

“And what?”

Seraphine rolled her eyes. “And are you going?”

The question hung between them, heavy as the coming storm. Elias flexed his fingers, watching the way the lamplight played over the scars on his knuckles—old wounds, most of them, but some still tender. “I don’t know.”

“Bullshit.” She leaned forward, elbows on her knees. “You’ve already decided. You’re just trying to talk yourself out of it.”

He wanted to deny it. Wanted to lie and say he was weighing his options, considering the consequences. But Seraphine had always seen through him, even when he was lying to himself. “It’s a trap,” he said instead.

“Probably.”

“He’ll have men there. Waiting.”

“Undoubtedly.”

Elias shot her a look. “You’re not helping.”

She shrugged. “I’m not here to help. I’m here to make sure you don’t get yourself killed.” Her fingers stilled on the knife hilt. “You go in there alone, you’re dead. You know that.”

“I know.”

“So don’t go alone.”

The words settled over him like a noose. Elias closed his eyes. He could still hear the echo of his father’s voice, rough with age and whiskey: *A man’s got to stand on his own two feet, boy. No one else is gonna do it for him.* But his father had been wrong about a lot of things. About honor. About loyalty. About the kind of man Elias was supposed to be.

“I can’t ask you to do this,” he said quietly.

Seraphine scoffed. “You’re not asking. I’m telling.” She stood, brushing hay from her trousers. “We leave in an hour. That gives you just enough time to pack and say whatever sentimental goodbyes you need to make.”

Elias stared at her. “You’re serious.”

“Dead serious.” She smirked. “Pun intended.”

He should have argued. Should have insisted she stay behind, safe. But the truth was, he didn’t want to go alone. And if he was being honest with himself, he never had.

“Fine,” he said, pushing to his feet. The hay crackled under his boots. “But if we die, I’m haunting you first.”

Seraphine’s laughter followed him down the ladder, bright and sharp as a blade. “Deal.”

---

The night air was bitter when they stepped outside, the kind of cold that seeped into your bones and stayed there. Elias pulled his coat tighter, the wool rough against his skin. The stable yard was empty, the other hands long since retired to the bunkhouse. Only the wind moved, rattling the loose shutters on the blacksmith’s shed, sending dead leaves skittering across the packed earth.

Seraphine adjusted the saddlebags on her horse, her breath misting in the air. “You sure about this?”

Elias checked the cinch on his own saddle, his fingers numb with cold. “No.”

“Good. Means you’re not completely stupid.” She swung up onto her mare, the animal shifting beneath her with barely restrained energy. “Let’s move. We’ve got a long ride ahead.”

Elias mounted, the leather creaking under his weight. The note was a lead weight in his pocket, burning through the fabric. He didn’t know what waited for them at the mill. Didn’t know if he’d walk away from this.

But he knew one thing for certain.

He wasn’t going to face it alone.

**The Weight of Silence**

The Night Court welcomed us home with a breath of cold wind, sharp as a blade against my cheeks. The sky above Velaris stretched like a bruise—deep purples bleeding into the black of midnight, the stars winking as if they, too, were holding their breath. The city below glittered, a jewel in the dark, but its light felt distant, untouchable. The weight of what we had left behind pressed down on my shoulders, heavier than any armor.

Rhys’s hand found mine, his fingers warm despite the chill in the air. His thumb traced slow circles over my knuckles, a silent promise. *I’m here.* But even his touch couldn’t ease the knot in my chest.

We landed on the terrace of the townhouse, the stone cool beneath my boots. The familiar scent of salt and night-blooming jasmine should have been a comfort. Instead, it only sharpened the memory of smoke and blood, of the way the battlefield had reeked of iron and fear.

Rhys released my hand, his wings folding neatly against his back. His voice was low, careful. “They’ll be waiting inside.”

I nodded, but my throat was too tight to speak.

The moment we stepped through the doors, the air inside the townhouse thickened. Cassian stood by the fireplace, his arms crossed, his jaw set in that way that meant he was one wrong word away from snapping. Azriel lingered in the shadows near the window, his face unreadable, though the tension in his shoulders spoke volumes. Mor was curled in an armchair, her usual vibrancy dimmed, her fingers worrying the edge of a throw pillow. And Amren—Amren sat at the table, her silver eyes sharp as knives, her small frame radiating barely contained fury.

No one spoke.

The silence was a living thing, coiling around us, suffocating. I could feel their eyes on me, their questions, their judgments. *What did you do? What did you sacrifice? Why didn’t you tell us?*

Rhys moved to stand beside me, a steady presence at my back. “We need to talk,” he said, his voice cutting through the quiet.

Cassian exhaled sharply. “No shit.”

Mor flinched. Azriel’s shadows flickered, restless.

I swallowed hard. “I know you’re angry.”

“Angry?” Amren’s voice was a whip crack. “You think this is about anger?” She stood abruptly, the chair scraping against the floor. “You went into that battle with half a plan and no warning. You risked everything—*everything*—and for what?”

“For the people we’re supposed to protect,” I snapped, my own temper flaring. “For the future of Prythian. Or did you think we could just sit back and let Hybern burn it all to the ground?”

Amren’s eyes flashed. “I think you don’t trust us. Not really.”

The words hit like a physical blow. I opened my mouth, then closed it. Because she wasn’t wrong. Not entirely.

Rhys’s hand settled on the small of my back, a silent anchor. “Feyre did what she had to do,” he said quietly. “And she did it to keep us all alive.”

Cassian scoffed. “At what cost?”

The question hung in the air, heavy and unanswerable.

I looked at each of them—their faces tight with worry, with frustration, with fear. These were the people who had stood beside me, who had bled for me, who had *chosen* me. And yet, I had kept them in the dark. I had made decisions that would shape their lives without giving them a say.

The weight of it threatened to crush me.

Rhys must have sensed the shift in me, because his hand pressed firmer against my back. “Give us a moment,” he said to the others.

No one argued. One by one, they filed out, leaving us alone in the quiet.

The door clicked shut behind them. The silence returned, thicker now, laden with everything left unsaid.

I turned to Rhys, my vision blurring. “What if they’re right?” The words tasted like ash. “What if I’m not—what if I’m not enough?”

Rhys cupped my face in his hands, his thumbs brushing away the tears I hadn’t even realized were falling. His eyes, those endless depths of violet and starlight, held mine with a steadiness that made my chest ache. “You are more than enough,” he murmured. “You always have been.”

I wanted to believe him. Gods, I wanted to.

But the doubt coiled inside me, cold and relentless. “I didn’t tell them,” I whispered. “I didn’t tell *you*. Not everything.”

Rhys’s expression didn’t change, but I saw the flicker of pain in his eyes. “I know.”

“I was afraid,” I admitted, my voice breaking. “Afraid that if I told you what I was planning, you’d try to stop me. Afraid that if I told them what I’d done, they’d look at me the way they’re looking at me now.”

Rhys pulled me against him, his arms wrapping around me like a shield. “You don’t have to carry this alone,” he said, his breath warm against my hair. “Not anymore.”

I buried my face in his chest, inhaling the scent of him—night and wind and something wild and untamed. For the first time since the battle, I let myself breathe.

But the weight of silence remained.

And I knew, deep down, that some things couldn’t be unspoken forever.

---

**Whispers in the Dark**

The fire in the hearth had burned low, its embers pulsing like a slow, dying heartbeat. I sat curled in the armchair, fingers tracing the edge of the book in my lap—pages unread, words blurring into nothing. The weight of silence pressed down on me, thick and suffocating, the kind that settled into bones and refused to be shaken.

I knew it couldn’t last.

The door creaked open, and Morrigan slipped inside like a shadow given form. Her dark hair was loose, spilling over her shoulders in waves that seemed to drink in the dim light. She didn’t speak at first, just leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, her golden eyes sharp as blades.

“You’ve been avoiding me,” she said, voice low.

I didn’t look up. “I’ve been reading.”

A lie. We both knew it.

Mor’s laugh was a dry, humorless thing. “Reading. Right.” She pushed off the doorframe and crossed the room in three strides, plucking the book from my hands before I could protest. The spine cracked as she flipped it shut, the sound too loud in the quiet. “You haven’t turned a single page in an hour.”

I exhaled through my nose, rubbing my temples. “What do you want, Mor?”

“For you to stop pretending you’re fine.” She tossed the book onto the side table, where it landed with a thud. “You think I don’t notice the way you flinch when someone touches you? The way you jump at every shadow? You’re not fine, Feyre. You haven’t been fine since—”

“Don’t.” The word came out sharper than I intended. I stood abruptly, the chair scraping against the floor. “Just… don’t.”

Mor didn’t back down. She never did. She stepped closer, close enough that I could see the flecks of amber in her eyes, the way her pupils dilated in the firelight. “I’m not your enemy,” she said softly. “But I won’t let you drown in this.”

“I’m not drowning.”

“Then why do you look like you’re one breath away from shattering?”

I turned away, my chest tight. The fire’s warmth licked at my skin, but it did nothing to thaw the cold knot in my stomach. “I don’t know what you want me to say.”

“The truth,” Mor said, voice steady. “For once.”

The truth.

The truth was that I woke up some nights gasping, my hands clawing at the sheets, convinced I was still back in that cell, still chained to the wall with Hybern’s knife at my throat. The truth was that I flinched when Rhysand reached for me, not because I didn’t want him to touch me, but because I was terrified of what I might see in his eyes when he did. The truth was that I didn’t know how to be *here*—not like this. Not when the war was over, and the world was supposed to be different, but I wasn’t.

I swallowed hard. “I don’t know how to do this.”

Mor’s expression softened. “Do what?”

“Live.” The word tasted like ash. “I don’t know how to live like this.”

She was quiet for a long moment. Then, slowly, she reached out and took my hand. Her fingers were warm, calloused. “You don’t have to have it all figured out,” she said. “But you can’t keep pushing everyone away.”

I wanted to argue. I wanted to tell her she didn’t understand, that no one did. But the look in her eyes—steady, unyielding—made the words die in my throat.

Before I could respond, the door swung open again.

Azriel stood in the doorway, his wings half-furled, shadows clinging to him like a second skin. His face was grim, his jaw tight. “We have a problem.”

Mor dropped my hand, her posture shifting instantly, all softness gone. “What is it?”

Azriel’s gaze flicked to me, then back to Mor. “Human lands. There’s movement—unrest. Armies gathering near the borders.”

My stomach twisted. “Hybern?”

“No,” Azriel said. “Humans. And not the kind we’ve dealt with before.”

Mor’s brows knit together. “What does that mean?”

“It means,” Azriel said, his voice low, “that they’re not just preparing for war. They’re preparing for *us*.”

The fire crackled, the sound suddenly too loud, too sharp. I looked between them, my pulse hammering in my throat. The peace we’d fought for—it had never been more than a fragile thing. And now, it was unraveling.

Mor exhaled sharply. “Rhys needs to hear this.”

Azriel nodded, but his eyes stayed on me. “He’s already on his way.”

I didn’t move. Didn’t breathe. The weight of it all pressed down on me, heavier than before. The war was over. But the shadows remained.

And they were whispering.

**The Unseen Enemy**

The war was over.

But the shadows remained.

And they were whispering.

The words slithered through my mind as I stepped into the dimly lit study, the scent of aged parchment and smoldering embers thick in the air. The fire in the hearth crackled low, casting flickering gold across the faces of the inner circle—each one tense, each one waiting.

Azriel stood near the window, his wings half-furled, the shadows clinging to him like a second skin. His report lay spread across the table, the ink still glistening where he had scrawled the final lines. The words were stark, unyielding: *Movement in the north. Not human. Not Fae. Something else.*

Cassian leaned against the edge of the desk, arms crossed, his jaw set in that familiar stubborn line. "We hit them now," he said, voice rough with barely restrained frustration. "Before they have a chance to dig in."

Amren’s fingers drummed against the armrest of her chair, the sound sharp as a blade against stone. "And risk another war?" Her silver eyes gleamed with cold amusement. "You’re as reckless as you were a century ago, boy."

Cassian’s wings twitched. "And you’re as cautious as a rabbit in a fox’s den."

Rhysand said nothing.

He stood apart from the rest of us, his back to the room, his gaze fixed on the city beyond the window. The Night Court sprawled beneath us, a sea of twinkling lights, but his silence was a void in the room, swallowing the tension whole.

I studied him—the way his fingers curled slightly at his sides, the way his shoulders held a stiffness that wasn’t there before. He knew something. Something he wasn’t saying.

My chest tightened.

"Rhys," I said softly.

He didn’t turn. Didn’t even flinch.

Amren’s lips curled. "He’s been like that since Azriel walked in."

Cassian exhaled sharply. "Great. Just what we need."

I ignored them, stepping closer to Rhys. The scent of him—night-blooming jasmine and something darker, something like storm clouds—wrapped around me, familiar and unsettling. "What aren’t you telling us?"

For a heartbeat, I thought he wouldn’t answer. Then, slowly, he turned. His face was a mask, but his eyes—his eyes were a storm of unspoken words.

Before he could speak, the door burst open.

A messenger stumbled in, her breath ragged, her clothes splattered with mud. The scent of damp earth and fear clung to her. "My lord," she gasped, bowing low. "The Spring Court—"

The words died in her throat as she saw me.

I knew that look. The way her gaze flickered over me, recognition sharpening into something like pity. My stomach twisted.

"Feyre," she whispered.

Cassian straightened, his wings flaring slightly. "What’s happened?"

The messenger swallowed hard. "The Spring Court is under attack. Not by humans. Not by Fae." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "By shadows."

The room went still.

Rhysand’s expression didn’t change. But his fingers curled into fists at his sides.

And I—

I felt the weight of my past pressing down on me, the ghosts of that court rising like specters in the corners of my mind. Tamlin. The estate. The life I had left behind.

The messenger’s next words were a blade to the chest.

"They’re saying it’s her fault."

Silence.

Then Cassian’s growl cut through the room. "The hell it is."

But Rhysand’s gaze was on me, dark and unreadable.

And I knew, with a sinking certainty, that the shadows weren’t just whispering anymore.

They were coming for me.

---

**A Choice Revisited**

The weight of Rhysand’s gaze pressed against my skin like a physical touch, heavy and unyielding. The air in the room had thickened, charged with something electric and dangerous. I could feel the shadows curling at the edges of my vision, not as threats, but as extensions of him—of *us*—stretching toward me with silent insistence.

I swallowed hard, my fingers tightening around the letter in my hand. The parchment was brittle, the ink smudged in places where my grip had trembled. The message from the Spring Court was a blade wrapped in silk, its edges sharp enough to draw blood if I wasn’t careful.

*She is not forgotten.*

The words echoed in my skull, a taunt, a reminder. A choice I had made long ago, one that had shaped everything since.

Rhysand didn’t move from where he stood by the window, his silhouette framed against the dim glow of the city below. The Night Court stretched out beneath us, a kingdom of stars and secrets, but even its beauty couldn’t distract me from the storm brewing inside me.

“You’re thinking too loudly,” he murmured, his voice a low rumble that vibrated through the space between us. “It’s giving me a headache.”

I shot him a glare, but the effect was ruined by the way my throat tightened. “This isn’t a joke.”

“I never said it was.” He turned then, his wings flaring slightly before settling against his back. The movement was deliberate, a reminder of the power he carried, the control. “But you’re spiraling, and I’d rather not watch you drown in it.”

I exhaled sharply, the letter crumpling further in my grip. “What if I made the wrong choice?”

The words hung between us, raw and ugly. Rhysand’s expression didn’t change, but his eyes—those endless, star-strewn eyes—darkened. “You don’t believe that.”

“Don’t I?” I stepped forward, my boots clicking against the obsidian floor. “What if I was selfish? What if I—”

“Feyre.” His voice was a whip-crack, sharp enough to silence me. “You saved lives. You saved *her* life. That’s not selfishness. That’s courage.”

I wanted to believe him. Gods, I wanted to. But the doubt coiled in my chest, a serpent waiting to strike. “And if it wasn’t enough?”

Rhysand closed the distance between us in two strides, his hands gripping my shoulders. His touch was warm, grounding, but the intensity in his gaze burned. “Enough for who? For *you*?”

I flinched. “I don’t—”

“You do.” His thumb brushed the edge of my jaw, tilting my face up to meet his. “You’ve spent years running from this, from the guilt, from the fear. But you can’t outrun it forever.”

The truth of his words hit me like a physical blow. I *had* been running. From the memories, from the choices, from the part of myself that still ached with the weight of what I’d done—and what I’d left undone.

The letter in my hand suddenly felt like a brand. I looked down at it, at the elegant script that had once been familiar, now twisted into something foreign and dangerous.

“What if they’re right?” I whispered. “What if I *did* fail her?”

Rhysand’s fingers tightened, not painfully, but insistently. “You didn’t.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know *you*.” His voice was fierce, unyielding. “I know the woman who stood in the snow and bled for strangers. I know the woman who faced Amarantha and didn’t break. And I know the woman who looks at me like I’m the only thing keeping her from shattering.” His thumb traced the line of my cheekbone, his touch achingly gentle. “You didn’t fail her. You *chose*. And that’s not something to regret.”

The tears came then, hot and sudden, spilling over before I could stop them. Rhysand didn’t look away. He never did. He just pulled me against him, his arms wrapping around me like a shield.

I buried my face in his chest, breathing in the scent of him—night and wind and something wild. “I’m scared,” I admitted, my voice muffled against his shirt.

“I know.” His hand cradled the back of my head, his fingers tangling in my hair. “But you don’t have to face it alone.”

I pulled back just enough to meet his gaze. The shadows around us pulsed, alive and restless, but there was no fear in them now. Only him. Only *us*.

The letter felt heavier than ever in my hand. I looked down at it, at the words that had once been a lifeline, now a chain. And then, with a slow exhale, I made my choice.

I let it fall.

The parchment fluttered to the floor, forgotten. Rhysand’s eyes followed it, then lifted to mine, questioning.

I squared my shoulders. “We’re not hiding from this.”

A slow, dangerous smile curved his lips. “No?”

“No.” I stepped back, my pulse steadying. “If they want a fight, they’ll get one. But not on their terms. On *ours*.”

Rhysand’s smile widened, sharp and triumphant. “There she is.”

And just like that, the storm inside me stilled. The shadows no longer whispered—they roared.

I was done running.

**Chapter 4, Scene 1**

The shadows didn’t just whisper anymore—they *roared*.

I stood in the heart of the Night Court’s throne room, the obsidian dais cold beneath my bare feet, the weight of the dark crown pressing into my brow like a brand. The air smelled of burnt incense and old magic, thick with the scent of my own resolve. Around me, the walls pulsed with faint, bioluminescent veins of light, as if the court itself breathed with the rhythm of my heartbeat.

I was done running.

Rhysand stood at the edge of the dais, his violet eyes sharp with something unreadable—pride, maybe, or caution. The bond between us hummed, a live wire of tension, but I didn’t reach for it. Not yet.

The others were there too—Mor, leaning against a pillar with her arms crossed, her golden skin flushed with frustration; Cassian, his wings half-spread, muscles coiled like he was ready to take flight at the first sign of trouble; Amren, perched on the arm of a chair, her silver eyes gleaming with something dangerously close to amusement.

And then there was Azriel. Silent, watchful, his shadows curling around him like smoke. He didn’t speak, but his presence was a weight all its own.

Mor pushed off the pillar, her voice cutting through the silence. “You’re really doing this, then?”

I met her gaze, unflinching. “I am.”

She exhaled sharply, her breath ruffling the dark curls framing her face. “Feyre, you can’t just—”

“I *can*,” I said, my voice steady. “And I will.”

Rhysand’s fingers twitched at his side, but he didn’t move. Didn’t speak. He knew me well enough to understand when to let me stand on my own.

Mor’s jaw tightened. “You’re risking everything. The Spring Court, the human lands—”

“The Spring Court can burn for all I care,” I snapped, the words tasting like ash on my tongue. The memory of Tamlin’s face, twisted with betrayal, flickered in my mind, but I shoved it down. “And the human lands? They’re already at war with themselves. This isn’t about them.”

Cassian let out a low whistle. “Damn, Feyre. You’re not holding back, are you?”

I ignored him, my focus locked on Mor. “You wanted me to stop running. You wanted me to *choose*. Well, I have.”

Her expression faltered, just for a second. “And what did you choose?”

I lifted my chin. “The truth.”

The word hung in the air, heavy and final. The shadows around me surged, as if responding to my declaration, and for a moment, the throne room darkened, the bioluminescent veins flickering like dying stars.

Rhysand finally moved, stepping forward until he stood at my side. His voice was quiet, but it carried. “Then we face it together.”

Mor’s eyes flicked between us, her frustration giving way to something else—resignation, maybe, or understanding. She knew better than to argue when Rhysand and I stood united.

Amren let out a dry chuckle. “About time.”

Azriel’s shadows coiled tighter, but he didn’t speak. He didn’t need to.

I turned to Rhysand, the bond between us flaring to life, warm and steady. “We start with the Spring Court.”

His fingers brushed mine, just for a second. “Then we prepare.”

The air in the room shifted, the tension easing into something sharper, more focused. The storm inside me had stilled, but the world outside was about to erupt.

And I would be ready.

CHAPTER 5
The Weight of Tomorrow

**A Council of Whispers**

The storm inside me had stilled, but the air in the war room was thick with something far more dangerous than lightning.

I stood at the head of the obsidian table, my fingers pressing into the cool, unyielding surface. The weight of every gaze in the room settled on my shoulders like a mantle I wasn’t sure I could bear. Rhysand leaned against the far wall, arms crossed, his violet eyes unreadable. He had given me this space—this moment—to lead. But the way his shadows coiled at his feet, restless and watchful, told me he was ready to step in if I faltered.

I swallowed. My voice, when it came, was steady. “We have a problem.”

The words hung in the silence. No one moved. No one breathed.

Then Cassian pushed away from the table, his wings rustling like the first warning of a coming storm. “That’s an understatement, Princess.”

Azriel stood beside him, his Siphons glowing faintly against the dim light filtering through the arched windows. The intelligence in his hands was a living thing, pulsing with urgency. “The human lands are in chaos,” he said, his voice low, measured. “Not just unrest. Not just fear. *Organized* chaos.”

Morrigan sat across from me, her dark eyes sharp as blades. She didn’t speak, but the way her fingers tapped against the table—once, twice—told me she already knew. Of course she did.

Amren’s silver eyes flicked between them, her lips pressed into a thin line. “And the Spring Court?”

A muscle in Cassian’s jaw twitched. “Tamlin’s playing his usual games. But this isn’t just about him. There’s something else. Something *older*.”

The word sent a chill down my spine.

Rhys’s shadows stirred, slithering across the floor like serpents testing the air. “You’re saying this isn’t just a rebellion,” he murmured. “This is a *movement*.”

Azriel nodded. “With teeth.”

The room tilted. I could feel the past pressing in—the weight of every decision, every life I’d taken, every life I’d failed to save. The scars on my back ached, a phantom reminder of the cost of war.

Morrigan’s voice cut through the haze. “You look like you’re about to be sick, Feyre.”

I met her gaze. “I’m fine.”

“You’re not.” Her tone was flat, unyielding. “But we don’t have time for your guilt right now.”

Amren let out a sharp laugh. “Listen to the seer, girl. The dead don’t care about your regrets.”

Rhys’s shadows coiled tighter, but he didn’t speak. He didn’t have to. I could feel his worry like a second heartbeat.

I straightened, forcing steel into my spine. “Then what do they care about?”

Cassian tossed a sheaf of papers onto the table. Maps. Reports. Names scrawled in blood-red ink. “They care about *us*. About what we represent. A world where humans don’t have to kneel.”

Azriel’s Siphons flared. “And they’re willing to burn everything to make sure that never happens.”

The room fell silent again, heavier this time. The scent of old parchment and iron filled my nose, the taste of fear sharp on my tongue.

Morrigan and Amren exchanged a glance—one of those silent conversations that made me feel like an outsider in my own court. Then Amren sighed, her fingers curling into fists. “We knew this was coming.”

Rhys pushed off the wall, his shadows retreating. “We just didn’t know it would be this soon.”

I looked at each of them—the warriors, the spies, the ancient powers who had seen empires rise and fall. And then I looked at Rhys, his face unreadable, his faith in me absolute.

I was High Lady of the Night Court. I had faced death and won.

But this—this was different.

This was war.

And I wasn’t sure I was ready.

**The Spring Court’s Gambit**

The words hung in the air between us, heavy as the storm clouds gathering beyond the windows of the Night Court’s war room. *I wasn’t sure I was ready.*

A knock at the door shattered the silence.

Rhys’s fingers tightened around the armrest of his chair, knuckles bleaching white beneath his skin. His voice was a low, dangerous hum. *"Enter."*

The door swung open, and the scent of blooming jasmine and damp earth rolled in ahead of the messenger—a faerie male with golden curls and a smirk that made my stomach twist. His Spring Court livery was immaculate, the embroidered vines along his sleeves shimmering faintly in the dim light.

*"Lady Feyre."* His voice was smooth, honeyed, the kind of tone that had once been familiar. Now, it made my teeth ache. *"His Highness, Tamlin of the Spring Court, sends his regards."*

Rhys didn’t move. Didn’t breathe. But the air in the room thickened, charged with the promise of violence.

I forced my hands to remain steady on the table. *"Does he."*

The messenger’s smirk deepened. He reached into his coat and produced a sealed letter, the wax stamped with the sigil of a rising sun. *"And an offer."*

I didn’t take it. Not yet.

The last time I’d seen Tamlin, he’d been standing in the ruins of his manor, his face a mask of cold fury, his voice raw with betrayal. *"You lied to me."* The words had cut deeper than any blade. And then he’d let me go.

I could still feel the ghost of his fingers around my throat.

Rhys’s chair scraped against the stone as he stood, slow and deliberate. The messenger’s smirk faltered for half a breath before he caught himself, straightening his spine.

*"You may deliver your message,"* Rhys said, his voice a velvet blade, *"and then you may leave."*

The faerie’s throat bobbed. He broke the seal with a flick of his wrist and unfolded the parchment, clearing his throat. *"‘To the Lady of the Night Court, Feyre Archeron—’"*

*"I know who I am,"* I interrupted, my voice sharper than I intended.

The messenger’s eyes flicked to me, then back to the letter. *"‘The Spring Court extends an invitation for diplomatic discourse, in the interest of fostering peace between our courts. Given our… shared history, I trust you will understand the necessity of this meeting. The borders of your lands grow restless, and the human territories beyond them are not as docile as they once were. It would be a shame if old wounds festered while new threats rose. I await your response.’"*

He lowered the letter. *"His Highness also requested I inform you that the invitation is not without… conditions."*

Rhys exhaled through his nose, a sound like a storm gathering. *"Get out."*

The messenger didn’t need to be told twice. He bowed—too deeply, mockingly—and backed toward the door. *"I’ll be at the inn in the city should you wish to send a reply."*

The door clicked shut behind him.

Silence.

Then Rhys was beside me, his hand warm on my shoulder. *"Feyre."*

I stared at the letter on the table. The wax had cracked when the messenger opened it, the edges jagged. Like broken glass.

*"He’s testing me,"* I said.

*"He’s provoking you."*

*"He wants to see if I’ll flinch."*

Rhys’s thumb brushed the nape of my neck, a silent anchor. *"And will you?"*

I thought of the last time I’d seen Tamlin. The way his court had looked at me—like I was a traitor. Like I was nothing. The way he’d refused to meet my eyes when I’d begged him to listen.

The way he’d let me walk away.

I picked up the letter. The parchment was thick, expensive. The kind of thing meant to impress. To remind me of what I’d left behind.

*"No,"* I said, my voice steady. *"I won’t flinch."*

Rhys’s fingers stilled. *"What are you going to do?"*

I met his gaze. His eyes were dark, endless, the only thing in the room that didn’t feel like a threat.

*"I’m going to write him back,"* I said. *"And I’m going to make sure he regrets ever thinking he could play this game with me."*

Rhys’s lips curved, slow and dangerous. *"Oh?"*

I reached for a fresh sheet of parchment, for ink, for a quill. My hand didn’t shake.

*"I’m going to tell him I accept his invitation,"* I said. *"And then I’m going to show up with an army at my back."*

**A Moment of Truth**

The door to our chambers clicked shut behind me, the sound too loud in the sudden silence. My fingers trembled as I turned the lock, as if that flimsy barrier could keep out the weight of what I’d just promised—*an army at my back.* The words echoed in my skull, sharp and relentless.

I didn’t let myself breathe until I was across the room, my back pressed against the cool stone of the far wall. The fire in the hearth had burned low, casting long, wavering shadows that stretched like grasping fingers across the floor. I slid down until I was sitting, my knees drawn to my chest, my arms wrapped tight around them.

And then, finally, I let it break.

The first sob tore from my throat before I could stop it, raw and ugly. My hands flew to my face, fingers digging into my skin as if I could claw the emotion back inside. But it was too late. The dam had cracked, and everything I’d been holding back—fear, doubt, the gnawing terror that I wasn’t enough—came rushing out.

I didn’t hear Rhys enter.

One moment, I was alone in the dark, and the next, his scent wrapped around me—night-blooming jasmine and frost-kissed wind. His presence was a quiet storm, filling the room without a sound. I kept my face buried in my hands, my shoulders shaking, but I felt him kneel before me. The heat of his body radiated toward mine, a silent offer of warmth.

“Feyre.”

His voice was soft, but it carried the weight of something unspoken. I swallowed another sob, my throat aching. I didn’t want him to see me like this. Not when I’d spent so long pretending I was strong enough to carry it all.

But he didn’t move. Didn’t speak again. Just waited.

The silence stretched between us, thick and suffocating. I lowered my hands, my fingers slick with tears. Rhys was crouched in front of me, his usual effortless grace replaced by something heavier. His wings were slightly spread, as if he’d flown here in haste, and his dark hair was tousled, like he’d been running his hands through it. But it was his eyes that undid me—the way they searched mine, not with pity, but with a quiet understanding that made my chest ache.

“Talk to me,” he murmured.

I let out a shaky breath. “I don’t know if I can do this.”

The words tasted like ash. I’d spent so long convincing everyone—*convincing myself*—that I was ready. That I could face whatever came next. But now, with the weight of my promise still fresh on my tongue, I wasn’t sure.

Rhys didn’t flinch. Didn’t offer empty reassurances. He just reached out, his fingers brushing a tear from my cheek. The touch was gentle, but it burned. “Tell me what you’re afraid of.”

I closed my eyes. “Everything.”

A beat of silence. Then, his thumb traced the line of my jaw, his touch achingly tender. “That’s not an answer.”

I opened my eyes. His face was so close to mine, his breath warm against my skin. I wanted to lean into him, to let him carry the weight for just a little while. But I couldn’t. Not yet.

“I’m afraid of failing,” I whispered. “Of making the wrong choice. Of not being strong enough to protect them—*you.*” My voice cracked. “I’m afraid of becoming her.”

The words hung between us, heavy with meaning. *Her.* My mother. The woman who had let fear rule her, who had let it destroy everything she loved. I could still see her face in my nightmares, pale and hollow-eyed, her hands shaking as she whispered, *“We can’t fight them, Feyre. We can’t win.”*

Rhys’s expression darkened. “You are nothing like her.”

“Aren’t I?” My laugh was bitter. “I stood in that room tonight and promised an army. An *army*, Rhys. What if I lead them to slaughter? What if I’m wrong?”

He didn’t answer right away. Instead, he shifted, settling onto the floor beside me, his shoulder pressing against mine. The contact was grounding, a reminder that I wasn’t alone.

“I’m afraid too,” he said quietly.

I turned my head, surprised. Rhys was always so sure, so unshakable. To hear him admit fear was like seeing the sky crack.

“Of what?” I asked.

His jaw tightened. “Of failing you. Of not being the High Lord you need. Of watching everything we’ve built crumble because I wasn’t strong enough to hold it together.” His voice dropped, rough with emotion. “I spent centuries believing I was alone in this. That if I fell, no one would catch me. And then you came along, and for the first time, I had something to lose.”

My breath hitched. I reached for his hand, my fingers threading through his. His skin was warm, his grip firm.

“We’re both afraid,” I said softly. “But we’re not alone in it.”

Rhys turned his head, his forehead resting against mine. His breath was steady, his presence a balm to the storm inside me. “No,” he murmured. “We’re not.”

I closed my eyes, letting the truth of it settle over me. The fear didn’t disappear. But it didn’t feel so heavy anymore.

When I opened my eyes again, Rhys was watching me, his gaze steady and sure. “Whatever comes next,” he said, “we face it together.”

I nodded, my throat tight. “Together.”

And when he pulled me into his arms, I let myself melt against him, his heartbeat a steady rhythm beneath my ear. The fire crackled in the hearth, the shadows dancing across the walls, but for the first time in days, I didn’t feel like I was standing in the dark alone.

We stayed like that for a long time, wrapped in each other, the weight of tomorrow pressing down—but not crushing us.

Not yet.

**The First Move**

The fire still burned low in the hearth, its embers glowing like dying stars against the dark stone. The warmth of Rhysand’s body pressed against mine, his arm a steady weight around my waist, anchoring me to the present. For the first time in days, the shadows in the room didn’t feel like omens—they were just shadows. But the peace was fragile, a thin veil over the storm gathering beyond these walls.

Rhysand’s breath stirred the hair at my temple. “We can’t wait any longer,” he murmured, his voice rough with the weight of what came next.

I knew what he meant. The reports had trickled in for weeks—whispers of unrest in the human lands, of villages burning, of something darker stirring beneath the surface. The Spring Court’s involvement was a tangle of old wounds and new threats, and if we didn’t act soon, the fragile balance we’d fought for would shatter.

I turned in his arms, my fingers tracing the sharp line of his jaw. “Then we send them.”

His grip tightened, just for a second. “Not *we*, Feyre.”

The words hung between us, heavy as the storm clouds gathering over the mountains. I pulled back, my pulse spiking. “You can’t expect me to stay here while—”

“While what?” His voice was low, dangerous. “While the people I love walk into danger? While the court you’ve bled for teeters on the edge of war?” His hands cupped my face, his thumbs brushing over my cheekbones. “You are the heart of this court. If you leave, if something happens to you—”

“Then I’ll be careful,” I snapped, stepping out of his reach. The firelight cast his face in sharp relief, his eyes glowing like molten silver. “I’m not some fragile thing you need to lock away, Rhysand. I’ve faced worse than this.”

“And I’ve lost you before.” The words were a blade, sharp and final. “I won’t do it again.”

The air between us crackled, thick with unspoken fears. I could taste the smoke from the hearth, bitter on my tongue, could feel the cold draft slipping through the cracks in the stone. My hands clenched at my sides. “You don’t get to decide this for me.”

“No?” His wings flared slightly, a dark storm against the dim light. “Then who does, Feyre? Who decides when the risk is too great?”

I wanted to scream. I wanted to shake him, to make him understand that this wasn’t just about me—it was about *them*. About the people we’d sworn to protect. About the future we were fighting for.

But before I could speak, he closed the distance between us, his hands gripping my shoulders. “I am not trying to control you,” he said, his voice raw. “I am trying to keep you alive.”

The fight drained out of me, leaving only the hollow ache of fear. I pressed my forehead to his chest, my breath coming in shallow gasps. “I can’t just sit here and do nothing.”

“I know.” His arms wrapped around me, his wings folding like a shield. “That’s why I’m sending Cassian and Mor. They’ll find out what’s happening. They’ll stop it before it reaches our borders.”

I pulled back just enough to meet his gaze. “And if they can’t?”

His jaw tightened. “Then we’ll deal with it. Together.”

The compromise settled over us, heavy and unsatisfying. I exhaled sharply, my fingers twisting in the fabric of his shirt. “Fine. But I want to be part of every decision. No secrets. No half-truths.”

His thumb brushed over my bottom lip, his touch achingly gentle. “You have my word.”

The tension between us didn’t fade, but it softened, like the first light of dawn breaking over the horizon. Rhysand pressed a kiss to my forehead, his lips lingering for a heartbeat before he pulled away.

Outside, the wind howled through the mountains, carrying the scent of rain and distant smoke. I followed him to the balcony, the cold stone biting through the soles of my boots. Below, the courtyard was a flurry of movement—Cassian’s massive frame already striding toward the waiting Illyrians, Mor’s dark hair whipping in the wind as she exchanged quiet words with Azriel.

Rhysand’s hand found mine, our fingers intertwining. “They’ll be careful,” he murmured, though the words sounded more like a prayer than a promise.

I didn’t answer. I couldn’t.

Because as the first of the Illyrians took to the sky, their wings cutting through the storm-laden air, I knew the truth: some risks couldn’t be avoided. Some storms couldn’t be outrun.

And we were standing right in their path.
